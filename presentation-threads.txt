## Description (3,498/5,000)

````markdown
## Why we built it

We saw frontier models get much better at 3D design, for example in Blender. We think it happened because someone built training environments for 3D work. FrameHunt is our training environment for video.

In our experience, models are still bad at video. When we want a model's help with a YouTube video, we end up pasting in the transcript. A transcript line like "okay so first click this one" doesn't say which button the speaker clicks.

## Why video is hard for a model

A 5-minute video at 10 frames a second is 3,000 frames. At full size, that is 6.2 billion pixels. So a model has to choose which frames to look at, which part of each one, and at what size. FrameHunt makes an agent practise this with a fixed pixel budget.

## How it works

The agent gets one question about a screen recording, for example "What is the request id on the 2nd ERROR line?". Our code generates the recordings. A recording shows a scrolling service log, a desktop app with error dialogs, or the same app with toast notifications.

- The agent talks to an MCP server. It asks for single frames, cropped or shrunk. Every pixel it gets back counts against a budget.
- The budget is 120,000 pixels per question. That is about 6% of one 1920x1080 frame.
- The right answer on the first submission scores 1. Anything else scores 0.

## How to solve it

1. Shrink the whole screen to 1/8 size. The log is a blur, but the error counter in the corner is readable.
2. Jump between frames, reading only the counter, until you find the frame where it turns 2.
3. Take one full-size crop of the ERROR line on that frame and read the id.

Our reference script does this with the same tools the agent gets.

## Results

We ran 30 questions, 10 per kind of video.

| Run | Correct | Average pixels per question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Our reference script | 30 of 30 | 49,776 |
| Opus 5.5, run 1 | 24 of 30 | 105,315 |
| Opus 5.5, run 2 | 17 of 30 | 107,405 |
| Opus 5.5, run 3 | 22 of 30 | 108,180 |
| Do nothing | 0 of 30 | 0 |
| One look, then guess | 0 of 30 | 32,400 |

On the log video, Opus gets 6, 1 and 6 out of 10. 25 of Opus's 27 wrong answers came with less than 10,000 pixels left.

## Why Opus fails

Opus asks for large images early. When it reaches the log, it has too few pixels left to check which frame it is reading.

We read 18 failures from our first round of runs. In 9 of the 14 log failures, Opus read the log before the ERROR line appeared. The frame it read has other request ids in the same format. Checking the counter on that frame costs a few hundred pixels.

In 17 of the 18 failures, Opus's final message says it is guessing. One of them says "The only image I could afford there was 12×7 pixels, which is unreadable, so I picked `f` with nothing to support it."

## Reward

- An agent that does nothing scores 0 of 30.
- An agent that takes one look and guesses scores 0 of 30.
- A blind guess at an 8-digit hex request id is right 1 time in 4.3 billion.
- The checker runs in its own container and never reads a file the agent wrote.
- Saving pixels earns no points. If it did, doing nothing would score.

## Run it

```
scripts/eval.sh
```

This one command runs the reference script, both baselines and three Opus runs, then writes the results table.

## What comes next
````

Someone unfamiliar with AI training could reasonably think you train models to generate videos, edit videos or understand lectures. They cannot yet picture what FrameHunt actually does.

A more concrete explanation would be:

> We built a game where AI finds answers inside screen recordings—and learns where to look.

You can introduce the technical term once they understand the activity.

**2. Blender takes a full slide before the audience knows your project.**

The clips are impressive, and I understand why you want them. But a reader might think you built those worlds, or that your project involves 3D generation.

The intended connection—practice environments help develop abilities, and you want to develop video skills—currently requires narration. Also, the two recent demos illustrate capability; they do not themselves demonstrate improvement over older models.

**3. The YouTube example establishes a different problem from the one you test.**

The lecture slide says: “A transcript misses visual information.”

FrameHunt asks: “How should an agent choose which moments and regions to inspect with limited resources?”

Those ideas connect, but the connection needs to appear:

> Understanding a video means finding the right moment and looking closely enough to read it.

Then explain that you start with generated screen recordings because you can automatically check the answers.

**4. The results arrive before the rules.**

On slide 4, the reader does not know:

- What the AI receives.
- How it explores the recording.
- What limits it.
- What counts as success.

Without that, the scores could look like ordinary screenshot-reading mistakes. **The interesting part—the agent deciding where and when to look—is invisible.**

**5. The final slide shows improvement without showing what was learned.**

“Zero to hero” has personality. But even with the blanks filled, the reader needs a concrete interpretation: did Qwen learn to locate events, choose better crops, conserve its viewing budget or simply read codes better?

One real before/after example would make the numbers much more meaningful.

### Three directions

**Option A: Keep your current story and restore one essential slide.**

This preserves the Blender opening and the visual style you like:

1. **FrameHunt:** what you built, plus one sentence explaining the challenge.
2. **Blender:** the inspiration, with an explicit connection to learning through practice.
3. **YouTube:** what transcripts leave out.
4. **How FrameHunt works:** one question, a recording, a few selected views, an answer.
5. **Where Opus struggles:** the three environments and scores.
6. **Qwen before and after training:** the payoff.

This is the smallest structural fix. The additional slide earns its place by making the results understandable.

**Option B: Lead directly with the problem and your project.**

This is my strongest choice for a deck people read without you:

1. **FrameHunt:** “We teach AI where to look in video.”
2. **The problem:** the lecture clip and the visual information missing from its transcript.
3. **The game we built:** demonstrate one task and its rules.
4. **What happens today:** Opus’s results and one concrete mistake.
5. **What training changes:** Qwen before/after, with a short explanation of the learned behavior.

Blender becomes optional material for the spoken presentation. The audience reaches your actual work much earlier.

**Option C: Open with a task the audience can try.**

Start with a short screen recording and a question:

> What code appeared on the third error message?

Then explain that the AI must find it by requesting selected images within a viewing budget. Follow with its attempt, the failure and the trained model’s improvement.

This has the most potential for fun. The audience understands the task by experiencing it. For readers, the slide must also contain selected still frames so the story survives without playback.

### Ideas I would use in any version

**Show one complete attempt.** A simple sequence could carry most of the explanation:

> Question → small overview → find the event → sharp crop → answer

Put the viewing-budget rule directly beside it. You do not need a separate technical lecture or lots of small captions.

**Make the challenge understandable in one sentence.**

> Our challenge: build a task today’s best AI agents struggle with, then show how to do better.

That gives unfamiliar readers a reason to care about the failures.

**Use a visible mistake for humour.** For example, show the frame Opus inspected beside the frame containing the answer, if supported by the selected run. “One frame too early” is understandable and memorable. A funny, documented mistake gives the project personality.

**Keep the newspaper styling.** It supports the clean look you want. Use the large headlines to explain the story; essential meaning should never depend on tiny grey text.

**Separate the achievement from the ambition.** Screen-recording results demonstrate progress on FrameHunt. Better understanding of arbitrary YouTube videos is the broader motivation. Making that relationship explicit helps readers trust the pitch.

My recommendation is **Option B for the submitted deck**, or **Option A if keeping Blender first remains a priority**. The key addition is the same: let readers see an agent actually playing FrameHunt before asking them to interpret its score.
